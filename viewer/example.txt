Why Night Trains Are Coming Back to Europe

For most of the twentieth century, overnight trains were an ordinary way to cross Europe, carrying businesspeople, families and soldiers between capitals while they slept. By the early 2000s, however, many state railways had quietly closed their sleeper services, arguing that low-cost airlines and high-speed lines had made them too expensive to run and too slow to compete.

The revival began in 2016, when the Austrian operator ÖBB took over several routes that Deutsche Bahn had abandoned and rebranded them as Nightjet. Instead of treating the trains as a loss-making relic, ÖBB invested in new carriages with private pods and modern compartments, and it marketed the service to travellers who wanted to avoid short-haul flights for environmental reasons.

Demand has been strong, but the economics remain difficult. A sleeper carriage holds far fewer passengers than a day coach, track access charges are often billed separately in every country the train crosses, and the rolling stock spends most of the daytime parked in a depot, earning nothing. Several new private operators, such as European Sleeper, have therefore started with second-hand carriages and only a few departures a week.

Supporters argue that governments should treat night trains as part of climate policy rather than as ordinary commercial products, for example by lowering track charges or coordinating timetables across borders. Critics reply that the money would cut more emissions if it were spent on improving daytime regional services, which carry far more people.
